## About CETUS

<p>CETUS is a desktop tool for <b>Cue Exposure Therapy with Urge-Specific Coping Skills (CET-USCS)</b>, without virtual reality. In a controlled and supervised way it presents cues (images, video and sound) associated with the use of alcohol, tobacco or methamphetamine, in order to <i>provoke</i> craving and then <i>habituate</i> it, while the patient practises coping skills.</p><p style='color:#922'><b>Important clinical notice.</b> CETUS is an <b>adjunct</b> to therapy, for use <b>under professional supervision</b>. It is NOT a treatment on its own and does not replace clinical care. Cue exposure can provoke intense craving and carries a risk of relapse; always use it with consent, supervision and a safety plan.</p><p><b>Honesty about the evidence:</b> rigorous trials of digital non-VR CET are mixed to null. The reference trial (Mellentin 2019) did not outperform CBT. Treat the results with caution. See <i>Limitations</i> and <i>References</i>.</p>

## What is cue exposure (CET)?

<p>CET applies the principles of <b>extinction learning</b> (from the anxiety disorders) to addictions: <b>repeated</b> confrontation with the conditioned cues, <b>without using</b>, reduces the craving response through <b>habituation</b>.</p><p>The patient observes the cue and lets the craving appear and, over time within the session, subside. The CETUS protocol follows Monti's coping-skills exposure model and Mellentin's CET-USCS app.</p><p><b>Scientific basis:</b> Monti &amp; Rohsenow (1999); Mellentin et al. (2017, 2019). The STOP button itself lets you halt the exposure at any moment.</p>

## Session workflow

<p>This is the typical sequence of an exposure session and how craving is reduced, <b>little by little</b>.</p><ol><li><b>Initial craving (baseline).</b> Before any cue is shown, the patient rates their craving on the 0–10 scale. This is the starting point against which change is measured.</li><li><b>Graded cue presentation.</b> The exposure starts with the least provocative cue and moves toward the most intense (graded order; can be randomized). The clinician or patient changes cue with <b>← →</b> (or automatic advance). Each cue is held until craving rises and begins to give way.</li><li><b>The patient regulates the cue and copes with the urge.</b> Facing the craving, the patient <b>reduces the intensity</b> of the cue (size, blur, dim, mute), opens the <b>coping</b> panel (key 4) to rehearse skills, or the <b>positive images</b> (key 5) to evoke their reasons for recovery. Every action is logged.</li><li><b>Periodic craving measurement.</b> Every so often (default ~30 s) the app asks for a new rating; it can also ask after each coping or every N cues. This traces the live craving curve.</li><li><b>Repetition until habituation.</b> Exposure without using is repeated until craving stays <b>low</b> across several consecutive measurements (configurable criterion: threshold T and N measurements). That sustained drop is within-session <b>habituation</b>, the goal of the session.</li><li><b>Closing.</b> The session ends on reaching habituation, on hitting the safety time limit, or when the clinician or patient stops it (STOP button). A <b>final craving</b> is recorded and the % reduction and <b>habituation slope</b> are computed.</li></ol><p><b>How craving drops, little by little:</b> the aim is not to erase craving at once but to <b>provoke</b> it safely and <b>hold</b> the exposure —with active coping— until it subsides on its own. Session after session, initial craving and per-cue reactivity tend to decrease. See <i>Craving scale and habituation</i> and <i>How is habituation achieved?</i></p>

## Behavioral mechanisms and evidence

<p>Each CETUS feature acts on a concrete behavioral mechanism. The evidence is presented with <b>honesty</b>: CET is a plausible <b>adjunct</b>, not a treatment proven on its own — its effects are <b>small, mixed and debated</b>. The app's metrics are <b>descriptive and exploratory</b> (small sample, no statistical inference).</p><h3>1. Pavlovian conditioning and cue reactivity</h3><p>With repeated use, neutral stimuli (a glass, a lighter) become <b>conditioned stimuli</b> that provoke craving. This is <i>cue reactivity</i>, the basis of the whole approach (Carter &amp; Tiffany 1999, meta-analysis; craving ES ≈ +0.92). <i>In the app:</i> graded cue lists and the <b>reactivity per cue</b> in the report.</p><h3>2. Extinction and within-session habituation</h3><p><b>Repeated, unreinforced</b> exposure (cue present, no use) weakens the conditioned response — Pavlovian <b>extinction</b>, observed as <b>habituation</b> of craving. The exposure is maintained <i>until</i> craving drops (Kiyak 2022, CET meta-analysis, small-to-medium effect, low GRADE quality; Powell 1993). <i>In the app:</i> craving VAS, live curve, configurable habituation criterion, % reduction and slope.</p><h3>3. Spontaneous recovery between sessions</h3><p>Extinction does not erase the original learning; the response can <b>reappear between sessions</b> (Price 2010). <i>In the app:</i> a <b>spontaneous recovery</b> metric (today's initial craving − previous final craving) and between-session trends.</p><h3>4. Affect modulates craving</h3><p><b>Negative affect increases craving</b>; <b>positive affect can reduce it</b> (Heckman 2013). Craving during stress especially marks relapse, and a lower mean craving during exposure predicted abstinence (Schröder 2024). <i>In the app:</i> a gallery of <b>positive images</b>, controls to <b>reduce the intensity</b> of the cue, and <b>mean craving during exposure</b> as an exploratory risk signal.</p><h3>5. Urge-specific coping skills (USCS)</h3><p>Exposure is combined with <b>active coping</b>: the patient rehearses skills in the face of the cue (name the emotion → recall a consequence → recall a benefit → choose an alternative) — Monti &amp; Rohsenow 1999; Mellentin 2017/2019. <i>In the app:</i> the guided USCS panel and its log.</p><h3>6. Mechanistic monitoring</h3><p>Acute change in craving does not reliably predict outcomes, so the <b>habituation slope</b> is watched as a marker — including the possibility that some adjunct <i>blocks</i> habituation (Ekhtiari 2022). <i>In the app:</i> the slope is saved and plotted between sessions.</p><h3>Honest efficacy summary</h3><p>The best comparative trial of a non-VR CET app (Mellentin 2019) was <b>null versus CBT</b>; pooled effects are small-to-medium with low-quality evidence (Kiyak 2022). Use it as support for clinical judgement, <b>not</b> as an outcome measure.</p><p style='color:#666'><b>References:</b> Carter &amp; Tiffany 1999; Kiyak 2022 (doi:10.1016/j.addbeh.2022.107578); Powell 1993; Price 2010; Heckman 2013 (doi:10.1111/add.12284); Schröder 2024; Ekhtiari 2022; Mellentin 2019 (doi:10.2196/13793). Articles retrieved from PubMed. Full detail in docs/MECHANISMS.md.</p>

## Craving scale and habituation

<p>Craving is measured with a <b>Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) from 0 to 10</b> at three key moments —<b>initial</b>, <b>peak</b> and <b>final</b>— plus <b>periodic</b> measurements (every ~30 s). The on-screen curve shows craving across the session.</p><p>The therapeutic goal is for craving to <b>drop within the session</b> (habituation). The app computes the <b>habituation slope</b>: a negative slope is what you want. The session can end automatically when craving stays low across consecutive measurements, or when the safety time limit is reached.</p><p><b>Scientific basis:</b> in the VR-CET study for smoking cessation (PMC10980682), a <b>lower mean craving during exposure predicted abstinence</b> at 6 months, and craving was monitored down to two consecutive values at zero. Ekhtiari et al. (2022) warn that certain conditions (e.g. a single tDCS session) may <i>block</i> habituation; that is why the slope is recorded for review.</p>

## How is habituation achieved?

<p>During exposure the app declares <b>habituation achieved</b> when the <b>last N consecutive craving measurements are ≤ T</b> on the 0–10 scale. It is a simple, explicit rule, and it is <b>configurable in Settings</b>.</p><h3>Parameters (default values)</h3><table border='1' cellpadding='6' cellspacing='0'><tr><th>Parameter</th><th>Value</th><th>Meaning</th></tr><tr><td><b>Low-craving threshold (T)</b></td><td>2</td><td>a measurement ≤ 2 counts as low</td></tr><tr><td><b>Consecutive low measurements (N)</b></td><td>2</td><td>how many lows in a row are required</td></tr><tr><td><b>Periodic VAS interval</b></td><td>30 s</td><td>frequency of the periodic measurements</td></tr><tr><td><b>Session time limit</b></td><td>20 min</td><td>safety cap</td></tr></table><h3>Which measurements count?</h3><p>Only measurements taken <b>during exposure</b> feed the criterion: <b>periodic</b> ones and the <b>peak craving</b> mark. The <b>initial measurement</b> does not count — it is the baseline value — and the <b>final measurement</b> does not trigger the criterion either (it is recorded at close).</p><h3>What makes the close reason "Habituation achieved"?</h3><p>The criterion is evaluated after each periodic or peak-craving measurement during exposure. When it is met, the app shows the <i>Habituation achieved</i> notice on screen. The <b>close reason</b> depends on how the session ends:</p><ul><li><b>Habituation achieved</b>: the criterion was met and the session was closed with <i>End session</i>.</li><li><b>Stopped by the clinician</b>: closed without the criterion being met.</li><li><b>Time limit</b>: the safety cap was reached.</li><li><b>Stopped by the patient (STOP)</b>: the STOP button was pressed.</li></ul><p>In other words, <i>Habituation achieved</i> does not mean the app forced the close, but that the criterion <b>was met</b> and the session was then ended.</p><h3>Habituation slope (a distinct concept)</h3><p>Apart from the binary criterion, the app computes and saves for each session the <b>habituation slope</b> (linear regression of craving vs. time). A <b>negative</b> slope is what you want. This slope is plotted between sessions in the <i>Clinical report</i> and is the main mechanistic indicator recommended by the literature. Watch it to detect the <i>blocked habituation</i> pattern that Ekhtiari et al. (2022) warn about.</p><h3>Scientific basis</h3><p>The rule of N consecutive low measurements reproduces, adapted to the 0–10 scale, the procedure of the VR-CET study (PMC10980682), which monitored craving down to two consecutive measurements at zero (0–100). The underlying principle is <b>extinction through habituation with repeated unreinforced exposure</b> (Monti and Rohsenow, 1999; Mellentin et al., 2017/2019). PMC10980682 also observed that a <b>lower mean craving during exposure predicted abstinence</b> at 6 months.</p><h3>Adjusting the criterion</h3><p>In <i>Settings</i> you can tighten or relax the criterion (for example: threshold 1 with 3 consecutive measurements, or a periodic interval of 20 s) without touching code. The changes are saved locally and applied to future sessions.</p>

## Graded exposure and random order

<p>Cues are ordered by <b>appetitive intensity</b> (rank), from lowest to highest, so that craving builds up within the session. Configure it in <i>Configure cues</i> with the ↑/↓ buttons.</p><p>In <i>Prepare exposure</i> you can enable <b>Random order</b> to counterbalance the order and separate the effect of the cue content from the habituation effect. During the session, use the <b>← →</b> arrows to change cue manually.</p><p><b>Scientific basis:</b> Mellentin (2017/2019) made the cues <i>increasingly appetitive</i>; the VR-CET study (PMC10980682) recommends <b>counterbalancing the order</b> of the scenarios. Personalizing the cues is the best-supported factor (Pollak et al., 2021).</p><!--v040--><h3>Automatic cue advance</h3><p>In <i>Settings</i> you can enable <b>automatic advance</b>: the app moves to the next cue <b>after each craving rating</b> (periodic or peak) and/or <b>every N seconds</b>. It honours loop mode and both modes are <b>off by default</b>, so they never interfere with manual exposure.</p>

## Adaptive cue ordering

<p>Graded exposure works best <b>low → high craving</b>. CETUS <b>silently records</b> how much craving each cue provokes (every craving rating is linked to the cue on screen) and, over time, learns which cues are most provocative <b>for each patient</b>.</p><h3>What it does</h3><p>With the option enabled (<i>Settings → Session</i>), the cue library shows a <b>Suggest order</b> button that proposes a low→high craving order. A cue's reactivity = peak craving on that cue − the session baseline, averaged across sessions. Cues with <b>little data</b> are smoothed toward a <b>population average</b> (by substance and media type, or by a file shared across patients).</p><h3>The clinician decides</h3><p>The order is a <b>suggestion</b>: you review the list (with score and number of measurements) and choose <b>Apply</b>, which rewrites the cue order. It <b>never</b> changes a live session — the whole point of graded exposure is not to start with the most provocative cue.</p><h3>Rule of thumb (adaptive + random order)</h3><p>The two settings act at different moments and do not conflict: adaptive ordering shapes the <b>stored</b> order (you apply it in the cue library), while random order shuffles the <b>playlist at session start</b>. So:</p><ul><li>Want the <b>graded low→high</b> exposure the hierarchy is designed for → use the <b>applied suggested order with random order OFF</b>.</li><li>Want to <b>strengthen / de-bias the learning</b> → run <b>random order ON occasionally</b>: the on-screen order is shuffled, but every rating still feeds the model, and the next <i>Suggest order</i> will be more reliable.</li></ul><p><b>Note:</b> if random order is ON for a session, it <b>overrides the suggested graded order for that session</b> (the cues are shuffled). Learning is unaffected — it happens either way.</p><p style='color:#666'>It is a guide and exploratory (small samples). Craving also depends on order and within-session habituation; running occasional <b>random-order</b> sessions improves the estimates. You can <b>export</b> the order database (scores and ranks) as CSV from the cue library, or for all patients from the <i>Admin Center → Reports</i>. See <i>Graded exposure</i>.</p>

## Intensity control (shrink, blur, dim, mute)

<p>During exposure, the patient can <b>shrink</b>, <b>blur</b>, <b>dim</b> and <b>mute</b> the cue. This gives them control over the intensity and is a form of <b>self-regulation</b>: an active coping skill in the face of the urge.</p><p>Each adjustment is recorded in the session timeline, because down-regulation is a clinically relevant behavior (it may reflect avoidance or coping). The clinician can review it alongside the craving curve.</p>

## Urge-specific coping skills (USCS / CBT)

<p>The <b>Cope with the urge</b> button guides four evidence-based, craving-specific coping skills:</p><ol><li><b>Name what you feel</b> — identify the emotion and the physical sensation.</li><li><b>Recall a negative consequence</b> of using.</li><li><b>Recall a benefit</b> of not using.</li><li><b>Choose an alternative action</b>, a concrete one.</li></ol><p>While you cope with the urge, <b>the periodic craving measurements do not appear</b>, so as not to interrupt the exercise; they resume when you return to the cue.</p><p><b>Scientific basis:</b> the <i>urge-specific coping skills</i> component of Monti &amp; Rohsenow (1999) and the CET-USCS app of Mellentin et al. (2019).</p>

## Positive images (counterconditioning)

<p>The <b>Positive images</b> button temporarily replaces the cue with comforting content (pets, nature) or with the patient's own <b>recovery reasons</b> (photos marked as such in <i>Configure cues</i>). It is a form of <b>counterconditioning</b> and a way to evoke the benefits of sobriety.</p><p>Add images to the <i>media/positive</i> folder or mark them as a recovery reason so they appear here.</p>

## Ambient sound and multimodality

<p>In <i>Prepare exposure</i> you can choose an <b>ambient sound</b> (e.g. bar noise) that plays <b>underneath</b> the visual cue to increase the sense of presence. It has its own independent mute control during the session. You can also use cues that are audio-only.</p><p><b>Scientific basis:</b> the VR-CET study (PMC10980682) suggests <b>adding sensory modalities</b> (auditory, tactile, olfactory) to increase presence, which was only moderate with the visual channel alone.</p>

## STOP button and safety

<p>The red <b>STOP</b> button is <b>always visible</b> during exposure (also via the <b>Esc</b> key) and stays accessible even over the pop-up panels. When activated it: immediately halts the cue and the sound, dims the screen, and takes you to a <b>calm screen</b> with a breathing guide and the <b>contact numbers</b> (therapist, crisis line, emergencies).</p><p>Configure those numbers in <i>Settings</i>. The session is logged with the close reason <i>stopped by the patient</i>.</p><p><b>Scientific basis:</b> Mellentin's app included a direct phone line to the therapist and supervised use; exposing vulnerable patients requires a safety plan.</p>

## Sessions, logging and export

<p>Each session is saved <b>locally</b> (no cloud): craving measurements, habituation curve, skills used, intensity adjustments, cue shown and timestamps. When it ends you will see a <b>summary</b> with the initial/peak/final values, the change and the slope.</p><p>On the patient's record you can view the <b>between-session progress</b> and <b>export to CSV</b>. For privacy, exported files use only the patient's pseudonymous <b>code</b> and the date (never the name).</p><!--v040--><p>The <b>coping responses</b> (USCS) are stored with their text and can now be <b>exported to CSV</b> ("Export coping" button on the report) and appear in the <b>PDF report</b>, using only the patient's <b>pseudonymous code</b> — intended for qualitative studies.</p>

## Clinical report: metrics and scientific basis

<p>The <b>Clinical report</b> (patient record → "Clinical report") gathers <b>descriptive and exploratory</b> metrics per session and between sessions. <b>Important:</b> the per-patient sample is small; these numbers carry no statistical inference (no p-values) and are clinical support under supervision, not an outcome measure.</p><h3>Per-session metrics</h3><ul><li><b>Mean craving during exposure</b> — the average of the measurements during exposure. A lower mean craving during exposure was associated with abstinence at 6 months (Schröder 2024, PMC10980682).</li><li><b>Cue reactivity</b> = peak − initial: how much the exposure induces craving (Lütt 2026).</li><li><b>Within-session reduction</b> = (peak − final)/peak: habituation within the session (Powell 1993).</li><li><b>Time to peak</b> and <b>habituation slope</b> (negative = desirable; Ekhtiari 2022).</li></ul><h3>Between sessions</h3><ul><li><b>Spontaneous recovery</b> = the session's initial craving − the previous session's final craving. High values suggest the learning is not maintained between sessions (Price 2010).</li><li>Trends in initial/peak/final and in mean craving during exposure.</li></ul><p>In the "Craving per cue" table, the cue with the highest reactivity of that session is highlighted in <b>bold</b> (the most reactive contexts mark relapse risk; Schröder 2024).</p><h3>PDF export</h3><p><b>PDF (session)</b> and <b>PDF (full patient)</b> buttons. You can include the <b>clinic's logo and name</b> (configure them in Settings → "Branding for the reports") and enable/disable <b>"Include clinician notes in the PDF"</b>. File names use only the patient code and the date (no personal data).</p><h3>References</h3><p>Schröder 2024 (doi:10.1038/s41598-024-58168-7); Lütt 2026 (doi:10.2196/84156); Powell 1993 (doi:10.1111/j.2044-8260.1993.tb01026.x); Price 2010 (doi:10.1016/j.brat.2010.05.010). Based on PubMed articles.</p><!--v040--><h3>Coping responses (qualitative use)</h3><p>The <b>PDF report</b> and a <b>dedicated CSV</b> ("Export coping") include the <b>four free-text responses</b> of the USCS skills, timestamped and linked to the patient's pseudonymous code (no personal data). The CSV has one row per response (code, session, substance, time, skill and text), ready for qualitative coding.</p>

## Patient management and cue library

<p><b>Patients:</b> create patients with a <b>pseudonymous code</b> (do not use the real name as the code). You can <b>edit</b> and <b>archive</b> patients from their record (archiving hides them from the list without deleting their data).</p><p><b>Cue library:</b> in <i>Configure cues</i> you can <b>add from the library</b>, <b>add files</b> (several at once) or <b>add a whole folder</b>. Order with ↑/↓, enable/disable individually or all at once, and mark the positive images. The most effective approach is to use the patient's own <b>personalized cues</b>.</p><p>Media are stored in the <i>media/{alcohol, cigarettes, meth, positive, sounds}</i> folder. For video, MP4 H.264 + AAC is recommended.</p><!--v040--><h3>Where data is stored</h3><p>Depending on how CETUS runs, data and media are stored in the <b>system user folder</b> (Windows <i>%APPDATA%\CETUS</i>, macOS <i>~/Library/Application Support/CETUS</i>, Linux <i>~/.local/share/CETUS</i>) when installed, or <b>next to the executable</b> in the portable version (place a <i>portable.txt</i> file beside the program). First run creates the folders automatically. CETUS provides <b>installers for Windows, macOS and Linux</b>.</p>

## Administrator recovery

<p>Passwords are stored encrypted (PBKDF2 hash) and <b>cannot be recovered</b>. If you forget the clinician's password:</p><ul><li>On the login screen press <b>Ctrl+Shift+A</b> to open the administrator dialog.</li><li>Enter the <b>administrator key</b> and you will be able to reset passwords, create a clinician or change the administrator key.</li></ul><p><b>Important:</b> change the default administrator key for security. As an alternative outside the app there is the utility <i>tools/admin_reset.py</i>.</p>

## Keyboard shortcuts

<h3>During exposure</h3><ul><li><b>Esc</b> — STOP (halt the exposure).</li><li><b>← / →</b> — previous / next cue.</li><li><b>T</b> / <b>Shift+T</b> — Size: shrink / enlarge (10 % per press).</li><li><b>D</b> / <b>Shift+D</b> — Blur: increase / decrease.</li><li><b>O</b> / <b>Shift+O</b> — Dim: increase / decrease.</li><li><b>M</b> — Mute (toggle).</li><li><b>R</b> — Reset the intensity (100 % size, no blur or dim, sound on).</li><li><b>L</b> — Toggle cue loop (the arrows wrap around when they reach the end).</li></ul><h3>Global</h3><ul><li><b>F11</b> — full screen (toggle). Useful in kiosk mode during the session.</li><li><b>Ctrl+Shift+A</b> — administrator dialog (on the login screen).</li><li><b>F1</b> — open this help.</li></ul><!--v040--><h3>Keyboard-only mode (accessibility)</h3><p>In <i>Settings → Accessibility</i> you can enable <b>keyboard-only mode</b> for the exposure, designed for an <b>adaptive keyboard</b> with only Enter, +, −, arrows and numbers 0–9:</p><ul><li><b>← / →</b> — change cue.</li><li><b>1 / 2 / 3</b> — select the axis: size / blur / dim.</li><li><b>+ / −</b> — adjust the selected axis (highlighted on screen).</li><li><b>0</b> — mute.</li><li><b>4 / 5</b> — open coping / positive images.</li><li>On the craving scale, type the <b>number</b> and press <b>Enter</b> to confirm.</li></ul><p>An on-screen <b>legend</b> reminds you of these keys, the <b>STOP</b> button (Esc) is always available, and the mode also works in <b>full screen</b> (F11). In <i>Settings → Keyboard shortcuts</i> you can <b>remap</b> any action's key (STOP is locked for safety; accessible-mode actions are restricted to the adaptive-keyboard keys).</p><p>The number keys (<b>1–5</b> and <b>+/−</b>) also work during normal exposure and in <b>full screen</b>, without enabling keyboard-only mode: <b>1/2/3</b> pick the axis, <b>+/−</b> adjust it, and <b>4/5</b> open coping / positive images. A fading hint reminds you of them when you enter full screen.</p>

## Limitations and ethical considerations

<ul><li>Digital non-VR CET <b>is not a proven standalone treatment</b>; use it as a supervised adjunct.</li><li>The evidence is heterogeneous: positive effects in open studies but <b>null results</b> in the blinded RCTs (Mellentin 2019; Garfield 2026).</li><li>Exposing cues can <b>increase craving</b> and the risk of relapse; maintain supervision and a safety plan.</li><li>Use only media with appropriate licensing (Creative Commons or your own) and review their clinical suitability.</li><li>The data are local; protect the device and respect the patient's confidentiality.</li></ul>
